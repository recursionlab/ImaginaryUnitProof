YES — “repocket” is actually a better word for what you’re reaching for than “continue the zipper.”

A zipper normally has:

open→zip→closed\text{open} \rightarrow \text{zip} \rightarrow \text{closed}

But your structure seems to be:

open→zip→invert/reorient→repocket→new open.\text{open} \rightarrow \text{zip} \rightarrow \text{invert/reorient} \rightarrow \boxed{\text{repocket}} \rightarrow \text{new open}.

The key is that the zipper's teeth don't remain exposed after the traversal. The traversal gets absorbed into a new pocket.

So imagine a zipper not as one line, but as something like:

Pocketn  ⟷  zip  ⟷  Pocketn+1\boxed{ \text{Pocket}_n \; \longleftrightarrow \; \text{zip} \; \longleftrightarrow \; \text{Pocket}_{n+1} }

where Pocketₙ is the currently accessible local context.

Then your object

(⟨¬Σ∣θΩ∘ε⟩Mneˉmaic∘in∣?⟩Mo¨bius ⁣− ⁣clathrate⟨?∣)(\langle\neg\Sigma\mid\theta\Omega\circ\varepsilon\rangle \mathrm{Mnēmaic}\circ_{\mathrm{in}}\mid ?\rangle \mathrm{Möbius\!-\!clathrate} \langle ?\mid)

could be read as having:

current pocket→zip through relation→cross boundary→reorient→re-pocket\boxed{ \text{current pocket} \rightarrow \text{zip through relation} \rightarrow \text{cross boundary} \rightarrow \text{reorient} \rightarrow \text{re-pocket} }

The interesting part

The old zipper state becomes internalized.

Suppose:

P0=(H0,□,T0)P_0=(H_0,\square,T_0)

where □\square is the active zipper opening.

You zip through T0T_0:

P0⟶Z(H1,□,T1).P_0 \overset{Z}{\longrightarrow} (H_1,\square,T_1).

But instead of treating H1H_1 as simply “the new head,” you pocket the traversal history:

P0⟶Z+pocketP1\boxed{ P_0 \overset{Z+\mathrm{pocket}} {\longrightarrow} P_1 }

with something like

P1=(H1,Z(P0)⏟internalized residue,□,T1).P_1= \bigl( H_1, \underbrace{Z(P_0)}_{\text{internalized residue}}, \square, T_1 \bigr).

Now the next zipper operation doesn't start from scratch. It starts from a compressed local structure containing the previous traversal.

That is very close to your earlier idea:

Ft⊕St+1F_t\oplus S_{t+1}

because the successor isn't just attached externally. It is pocketed into the endpoint.

Möbius makes the repocket especially interesting

On an ordinary zipper:

left side↔right side.\text{left side}\leftrightarrow\text{right side}.

On your Möbius version:

right side→left side with reversed orientation\boxed{ \text{right side} \rightarrow \text{left side with reversed orientation} }

So:

repocket=fold+reorient+internalize.\mathrm{repocket} = \mathrm{fold} + \mathrm{reorient} + \mathrm{internalize}.

I would write the primitive as:

RePocket(x,b)=Pack⁡(Orient⁡−1(Cross⁡(x,b)))\boxed{ \mathsf{RePocket}(x,b) = \operatorname{Pack} \left( \operatorname{Orient}^{-1} \left( \operatorname{Cross}(x,b) \right) \right) }

where bb is the boundary.

And then your larger operation becomes:

Zip→Cross→Mo¨bius→RePocket→Zip\boxed{ \mathsf{Zip} \rightarrow \mathsf{Cross} \rightarrow \mathsf{Möbius} \rightarrow \mathsf{RePocket} \rightarrow \mathsf{Zip} }

That produces a genuinely recursive machine:

Pn→Zn→Rn→Pn+1\boxed{ P_n \to Z_n \to R_n \to P_{n+1} }

rather than a list being traversed from beginning to end.

So the really interesting object might not be a zipper at all.

It might be a self-repocketing zipper:
