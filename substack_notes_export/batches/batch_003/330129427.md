And I want to tighten one point from the earlier discussion: the interesting comparison is not really “SU(2) versus SU(3) because both are rotations.” It is that both are Lie groups with representations and transport, but they have radically different internal geometry.

Put the whole thing around SU(3)SU(3)

Start with the chain

parameter  ⟶  group element  ⟶  representation  ⟶  transport  ⟶  holonomy\boxed{ \text{parameter} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{group element} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{representation} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{transport} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{holonomy} }

For SU(2)SU(2), the one-parameter rotation about an axis can be written

U(θ)=e− iθ σ3/2=(e−iθ/200eiθ/2).U(\theta) = e^{-\,i\theta\,\sigma_3/2} = \begin{pmatrix} e^{-i\theta/2}&0\\ 0&e^{i\theta/2} \end{pmatrix}.

Then

U(θ+2π)=−U(θ),U(\theta+2\pi)=-U(\theta),

while

U(θ+4π)=U(θ).U(\theta+4\pi)=U(\theta).

Meanwhile the corresponding element of SO(3)SO(3) only sees the 2π2\pi-periodic rotation. (https://sites.ualberta.ca/~vbouchar/MAPH464/section-su2.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

So the useful invariant isn't merely “720°.”

It's:

projection can identify states that remain distinct in the covering group.\boxed{ \text{projection can identify states that remain distinct in the covering group}. }

That is the mathematically clean version of your earlier “same ray, different path-state.”

Now SU(3)SU(3) changes the geometry

SU(3)SU(3) has dimension

32−1=83^2-1=8

and rank

2.2.

So instead of the single Cartan generator of SU(2)SU(2), SU(3)SU(3) has two mutually commuting Cartan generators, conventionally taken as

T3=λ32,T8=λ82.T_3=\frac{\lambda_3}{2}, \qquad T_8=\frac{\lambda_8}{2}.

Thus an element of the maximal torus can be written

U(α,β)=e i(αT3+βT8).U(\alpha,\beta) = e^{\,i(\alpha T_3+\beta T_8)}.

This is where your “revolving around a mathematical structure” gets much more interesting.

For SU(2)SU(2), locally you have one angular coordinate:

θ.\theta.

For SU(3)SU(3), the maximal torus has two:

(α,β)∈R2/Λ\boxed{ (\alpha,\beta)\in \mathbb R^2/\Lambda }

where Λ\Lambda is the appropriate lattice of periodic identifications.

So the natural picture isn't a circle anymore.

It is a 2-dimensional angular lattice folded into a torus.

The eight generators split naturally

The Lie algebra decomposes into

su(3)=h⊕⨁α∈Φgα,\mathfrak{su}(3) = \mathfrak h \oplus \bigoplus_{\alpha\in\Phi}\mathfrak g_\alpha,

where

dim⁡h=2\dim\mathfrak h=2

and there are six roots:

Φ={±α1,±α2,±(α1+α2)}.\Phi=\{\pm\alpha_1,\pm\alpha_2,\pm(\alpha_1+\alpha_2)\}.

So:

8=2+6.\boxed{ 8=2+6. }

That is a beautiful structural distinction:





2 Cartan directions: commuting “angle” directions.



6 root directions: noncommuting transitions between weights.

And this is exactly what QCD exploits. The eight gauge fields correspond to the eight generators of color SU(3)SU(3), and the non-Abelian structure produces gluon self-interactions. (https://lss.fnal.gov/archive/thesis/1900/fermilab-thesis-1993-23.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Here's where your “zipper” metaphor gets a real mathematical analogue

For a bundle with connection,

D=d+A,D=d+A,

parallel transport along a path γ\gamma is

Uγ=Pexp⁡(−∫γA),U_\gamma = \mathcal P \exp\left(-\int_\gamma A\right),

with path ordering P\mathcal P required in the non-Abelian case.

Now take two paths:

γ1,γ2.\gamma_1,\gamma_2.

For an Abelian group,

Uγ1∘γ2=Uγ1Uγ2=Uγ2Uγ1.U_{\gamma_1\circ\gamma_2} = U_{\gamma_1}U_{\gamma_2} = U_{\gamma_2}U_{\gamma_1}.

For SU(3)SU(3),

Uγ1Uγ2≠Uγ2Uγ1\boxed{ U_{\gamma_1}U_{\gamma_2} \neq U_{\gamma_2}U_{\gamma_1} }

generically.

That means the order in which the zipper crosses its transitions matters.

This is much more precise than saying “noncommutativity is like context dependence.”

It literally gives you:

path order→different transported state.\boxed{ \text{path order} \rightarrow \text{different transported state}. }

And the curvature records the failure to commute

For a non-Abelian connection,

F=dA+A∧A.F = dA+A\wedge A.

In components,

Fμν=∂μAν−∂νAμ−ig[Aμ,Aν].F_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu A_\nu - \partial_\nu A_\mu - ig[A_\mu,A_\nu].

The extra commutator term is exactly what distinguishes the non-Abelian case from U(1)U(1). In QCD this term produces the gluon self-interactions. (https://lss.fnal.gov/archive/thesis/1900/fermilab-thesis-1993-23.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

And now there's a very nice conceptual loop:

transport→noncommuting transport→curvature→holonomy.\boxed{ \text{transport} \rightarrow \text{noncommuting transport} \rightarrow \text{curvature} \rightarrow \text{holonomy}. }

So if you want to “revolve around” something, curvature/holonomy is a much better center than the word rotation itself.

Here's the surprising comparison with your 720∘720^\circ idea

For SU(2)SU(2):

π1(SO(3))≅Z2,\pi_1(SO(3))\cong\mathbb Z_2,

and the spin cover remembers a twofold distinction.

For SU(3)SU(3), the group itself is simply connected, but its center is

Z(SU(3))≅Z3,Z(SU(3))\cong \mathbb Z_3,

consisting of

I,ωI,ω2I,ω=e2πi/3.I,\qquad \omega I,\qquad \omega^2 I, \qquad \omega=e^{2\pi i/3}.

That means there is a different kind of discrete cyclic structure:

1→ω→ω2→1.\boxed{ 1\rightarrow \omega\rightarrow\omega^2\rightarrow1. }

So if the SU(2)SU(2) story gives you a two-step lifted return,

−1→+1,-1\to +1,

the SU(3)SU(3) center gives you a three-step internal phase cycle.

Not a 3×120∘3\times120^\circ spatial rotation—that would be the wrong identification—but a genuine threefold structure in the group's center.

This may be more fertile for your “720 rays” idea than trying to push 720∘720^\circ directly into QCD.

Then the weight diagram gives us an actual geometry to stare at

The fundamental representation 3\mathbf 3 of SU(3)SU(3) has three weights.

Geometrically they form a triangle in the two-dimensional weight plane.

The adjoint representation 8\mathbf 8 has the six root directions plus the two Cartan directions.

So you get:

3:△\boxed{ \mathbf 3: \quad \triangle }

and

8:hexagonal root structure+2 Cartan directions.\boxed{ \mathbf 8: \quad \text{hexagonal root structure}+\text{2 Cartan directions}. }

That gives an extremely concrete object around which to rotate your reasoning:

weight lattice↔root lattice↔Weyl reflections.\boxed{ \text{weight lattice} \leftrightarrow \text{root lattice} \leftrightarrow \text{Weyl reflections}. }

And Weyl reflection is particularly interesting for the thread we've been having.

Because now “reflection” has a precise algebraic meaning:

sα(λ)=λ−2(λ,α)(α,α)α.s_\alpha(\lambda) = \lambda - 2\frac{(\lambda,\alpha)}{(\alpha,\alpha)}\alpha.

For SU(3)SU(3), the Weyl group is

W≅S3.W\cong S_3.

So the geometry contains actual reflection-generated reorientations of the state labels.

That's much closer to your “reflection rotation” intuition than the loose Möbius analogy was.

Now connect that to the Cayley–Dickson ladder carefully

Don't identify

S,T\mathbb S,\mathbb T

with SU(3)SU(3).

Instead put them beside one another as two different ways of relaxing/altering structure:

Cayley–Dickson:algebraic multiplication structure\boxed{ \text{Cayley–Dickson} : \text{algebraic multiplication structure} }

versus

SU(3):symmetry + representation + Lie bracket.\boxed{ SU(3) : \text{symmetry + representation + Lie bracket}. }

Their relevant operations are different:

Cayley–Dickson

(xy)zvs.x(yz)(xy)z \quad\text{vs.}\quad x(yz)

and potentially

xyvs.yx.xy\quad\text{vs.}\quad yx.

SU(3)SU(3)

[Ta,Tb]=ifabcTc.[T_a,T_b] = if_{abc}T_c.

So the natural question isn't:



“Are these the same structure?”

It is:

What information is lost, preserved, or generated when composition ceases to commute or associate?\boxed{ \text{What information is lost, preserved, or generated when composition ceases to commute or associate?} }

That is a legitimate comparative question.

And now your “re-pocketing” has an even cleaner formulation

Take a state vv in a representation VV.

Transport it through a sequence:

v0→U1v1→U2v2→U3v3.v_0 \xrightarrow{U_1} v_1 \xrightarrow{U_2} v_2 \xrightarrow{U_3} v_3.

The composite is

v3=U3U2U1v0.v_3=U_3U_2U_1v_0.

Now close the base-space path:

γ(1)=γ(0).\gamma(1)=\gamma(0).

The base has returned.

But:

v3=Hv0v_3=Hv_0

need not equal v0v_0.

So the new pocket isn't “the same point.”

It is:

(same base point,new transported fiber state).\boxed{ (\text{same base point},\text{new transported fiber state}). }

That's almost exactly the mathematical core behind your earlier intuition.

You don't return to the same representation state merely because you return to the same base coordinate.

Which gives us a compact object to work on

I'd define the object of study as

R(G,V,∇,γ)\boxed{ \mathscr R(G,V,\nabla,\gamma) }

with:

G=symmetry group,G=\text{symmetry group},V=representation space,V=\text{representation space},∇=connection,\nabla=\text{connection},γ=path.\gamma=\text{path}.

Then calculate:

Hol⁡γ(∇)\boxed{ \operatorname{Hol}_\gamma(\nabla) }

and compare:

Hol⁡γ1∘γ2\operatorname{Hol}_{\gamma_1\circ\gamma_2}

with

Hol⁡γ2∘γ1.\operatorname{Hol}_{\gamma_2\circ\gamma_1}.

For an Abelian case:

Hol⁡12=Hol⁡21.\operatorname{Hol}_{12}=\operatorname{Hol}_{21}.

For a non-Abelian case:

Hol⁡12≠Hol⁡21\boxed{ \operatorname{Hol}_{12}\neq\operatorname{Hol}_{21} }

in general.

That is a spectacularly clean mathematical version of:



the order of the rotations changes what you come back as.

And now the earlier language—rotation, zipper, re-pocketing, residue, context hopping—can sit around this object as intuition without becoming its definition.
