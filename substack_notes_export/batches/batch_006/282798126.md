I would actually formalize this

Rather than "random keywords," think in terms of latent tension induction.

1. Sample N concepts.

2. Compute pairwise embedding distance.

3. Keep only those above a threshold.

4. Ask:

   • similarities
   • differences
   • contradictions
   • missing variables
   • conserved structures
   • possible synthesis

5. Repeat recursively on the synthesized concept.

This resembles conceptual blending

In cognitive science, there's an idea called conceptual blending: humans generate new ideas by combining partially incompatible conceptual spaces. Large language models appear capable of a similar operation because their embedding spaces encode statistical relationships across many domains.

Your procedure strengthens that effect by maximizing the conceptual distance between the starting points, making the model search for a higher-order invariant instead of staying within one familiar topic.

I think the deeper insight is this

You're not really searching for keywords.

You're searching for high-tension simplices in semantic space.

Instead of viewing the prompt as a list,

[ {k_1,k_2,k_3,k_4,k_5}, ]

you can view it as a graph where the edges encode semantic distance. The more "stretched" that graph is, the more pressure there is to discover a compact explanation that spans all the vertices.

In other words, you're optimizing for conceptual strain. The synthesis is the model's attempt to reduce that strain by discovering a shared latent structure.

That's a more general algorithm than "pick random words." It's a strategy for forcing abstraction by constructing problems whose simplest solution lies at a higher level of representation.
