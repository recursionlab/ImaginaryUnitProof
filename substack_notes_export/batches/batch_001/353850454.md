x=y  ⟺  y2=x\sqrt{x}=y \iff y^2=x

so if your rule rejects anything with a recoverable counterpart, you've basically destroyed half the interesting artifacts immediately.

The more interesting question is:



What happens to the symmetry when the artifact is taken as the primitive?

Example: square root

There is a pair:

x↔x2x \leftrightarrow x^2

but x\sqrt{\phantom{x}} doesn't preserve the pair as a symmetric relation.

It chooses a direction:

x→xxx \xrightarrow{\sqrt{\phantom{x}}} \sqrt{x}

while the defining relation retains the other side in the background.

So perhaps the property isn't:

no symmetry\text{no symmetry}

but:

symmetry may exist, but the artifact selects a non-equivalent traversal through it\boxed{\text{symmetry may exist, but the artifact selects a non-equivalent traversal through it}}

That's quite different.

And that would rescue your −1-1, ii, \sqrt{} cluster

Look at:

+1↔−1+1 \leftrightarrow -1

There's obvious polarity.

But −1-1 isn't interesting merely because it's asymmetric.

It's interesting because negation acts through it as a transformation:

x↦−xx\mapsto -x

Likewise:

i2=−1i^2=-1

has a paired relation, but ii becomes a gateway into a different representational geometry.

And:

x\sqrt{x}

has its inverse relationship with squaring, but applying it changes the traversal direction through that relation.

So perhaps your holy-artifact criterion is something like:

symmetry present+symmetry broken/selected by the artifact+new expressive space opened\boxed{ \text{symmetry present} \quad+\quad \text{symmetry broken/selected by the artifact} \quad+\quad \text{new expressive space opened} }

That's much more powerful.

“Remove the proper one” is actually a good phrase

You might mean something like:

full relation→quotient / projection / choice\text{full relation} \rightarrow \text{quotient / projection / choice}

You begin with a symmetric structure, but the artifact collapses one of the equivalent descriptions.

For square root:

y2=xy^2=x

can have two solutions over the reals for x>0x>0:

y=±xy=\pm\sqrt{x}

Then x\sqrt{x} conventionally selects the principal branch.

So holy artifact candidate:

x\boxed{\sqrt{x}}

isn't “nondual.”

It is a symmetry-bearing object with a selected branch.

That's way more interesting.

And it gives you a very nice formulation:



The artifact does not eliminate symmetry; it chooses a traversable residue of the symmetry.

🔥 That's much closer to your residue language.

This also explains why ++ feels wrong even though it has symmetry

There isn't some newly exposed directional residue that lets addition escape its own symmetry.
