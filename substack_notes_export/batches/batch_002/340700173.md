The Living Computer: An Introduction to the 'Cognition = Life' Hypothesis

1. A New Lens on Life

For centuries, the prevailing view of existence has seen the human brain as a solitary pilot navigating a mechanical, mindless body. We treated "intelligence" as an evolutionary luxury—a high-level perk reserved for creatures with complex nervous systems. However, we are currently witnessing a profound paradigm shift. The "Cognition = Life" hypothesis proposes that cognition is not a late-stage addition to the tree of life, but its very root.

In this visionary framework, the body is no longer a "container for a brain." Instead, we see the organism as a continuous processor of information, an active participant in its own creation. From the chemical signals of a single-celled bacterium to the complex reasoning of a philosopher, life is the manifestation of information in motion.

Vision StatementBiological processes are fundamentally computational. Life is not merely supported by information; it is a form of embodied computation known as autopoiesis—the self-generation of a living system. Cognition is the primary driver of existence, allowing an agent to interpret its environment and act to maintain its own identity against the forces of decay.

This perspective reveals a universe where the abstract rules of logic and the physical structures of biology are two sides of the same coin, woven together in a grand computational web.

2. The Building Blocks: Info-Computationalism

To grasp the "Living Computer" model, we must adopt the framework of Info-Computationalism. This theory suggests that the universe is not made of "stuff" in the traditional sense, but of informational structures and the computational dynamics that transform them. Crucially, as a biosemiotician would argue, information does not exist "in the wild" as an objective entity; it is always relational.

Term

Simple Definition for a Learner

Information

Structure for a cognizing agent; the way reality is represented and interpreted by an entity for the purpose of survival.

Computation

Information dynamics; the physical and logical processes by which one informational state is transformed into another.

Agent

An informational structure capable of acting on its own behalf—interpreting signals and "computing" survival behaviors.

When a cell interprets a change in chemical concentration, it is not merely reacting like a falling rock. It is performing a biosemiotic act: assigning valence (meaning) to a signal and computing a goal-directed response.

3. The Body as the Program: Morphological Computing

In the silicon computers we build, we maintain a strict divide between hardware (the physical chips) and software (the code). Nature rejects this separation. This is the essence of Morphological Computing.





The Body is the Program: An organism’s shape, density, and molecular arrangement are not just housing; they are active components of its intelligence.



Self-Generating Component Systems: As defined by systems biologist George Kampis, a living system is a "computer that re-writes itself." When the "software" (biological processes) executes, it physically builds and alters the "hardware" (the cells and tissues).



Efficiency Through Logic-in-Reality: By using its own morphology to solve problems—such as a cell membrane naturally filtering molecules based on shape—life avoids the massive energy costs of symbolic calculation.

If the body’s physical structure is itself a form of computation, we arrive at a startling realization: Geometry is thought. Every fold of a protein and every curvature of a cell is a calculation in progress.

4. Intelligence Without Brains: Basal Cognition

This brings us to the concept of Basal Cognition. Intelligence began long before the first neuron was ever formed. All living cells are "agential materials"—sophisticated entities that solve problems, remember past states, and coordinate with neighbors without a central brain.

Cells use bioelectric networks and somatic memory to store information about their environment and their own physical state, allowing them to engage in complex decision-making.

Case Study: The Single Cell Even a single-celled organism exhibits the foundations of high-level reasoning:





Sensing & Interpretation: Using chemical languages (like Quorum Sensing) to perceive and "discuss" environmental states with peers.



Goal-Directedness (Valence): Assigning positive or negative value to signals and making choices to move toward nutrients or away from toxins.



Somatic Memory: Storing past experiences within its physical structure to predict future environmental changes.

This "hidden" intelligence allows biological systems to act as distributed computers, far more resilient than any centralized silicon processor.

5. Life vs. Silicon: Three Critical Differences

While we use the word "computation" for both, biological systems and traditional computers operate on entirely different logical planes.

Distributed: Intelligence is spread across every cell and bioelectric network.

Adaptability

Adaptive: Self-organizing; the hardware and software evolve together in real-time.

Not Aware: Turing machines calculate regardless of energy or physical constraints.

Primary Goal

These differences allow life to be "resource-aware" and robust; a laptop fails if a single chip is cracked, but a living system can re-organize its computational substrate to heal and persist.

6. The Great Recursive Circle: From Physics to Logic

Nature organizes itself through a scale-invariant hierarchy. This chain of "intrinsic logic" shows how the universe builds complexity from the ground up, eventually circling back to understand itself.





Logic is the foundation of Mathematics.



Mathematics is the intrinsic logic of Physics.



Physics is the intrinsic logic of Chemistry.



Chemistry is the intrinsic logic of Biology.



Biology is the intrinsic logic of Cognition.



Cognition generates the agent’s Logic (which restarts the circle).

This hierarchy is recursive. Our evolved human cognition generates the formal logic we use to describe mathematics and physics. In doing so, our "mind" is not observing the world from the outside; it is the final step in a self-referential loop where nature uses logic to investigate the very physics that birthed logic.

7. Conclusion: Your Place in the Computational Web

By viewing life as computation, we bridge the gap between the "cold" equations of physics and the "warm" experience of the mind. You are a self-organizing tapestry of information processing, a masterpiece of natural engineering. This intelligence is not an accident of evolution—it is the force that makes evolution possible.

Under the Extended Evolutionary Synthesis (EES), we now understand that evolution is not just random mutation, but a goal-directed learning process. Nature is not just "filtering" the weak; it is "computing" increasingly complex forms of existence.

The Learner’s Toolkit: Key Insights





Cognition is Life: If a system is alive, it is cognizing. All organisms interpret signals and compute survival strategies.



Morphology is Mind: Your physical structure is an active participant in your intelligence, not just a container for it.



Evolution is Multi-Scale Learning: Nature "learns" across multiple scales—from the bioelectric networks of cells to the social intelligence of ecosystems—to create more competent agents.

The future of science lies in learning to speak the language of biological computation. As we do, we move closer to a unified understanding of our place in a living, thinking universe.
